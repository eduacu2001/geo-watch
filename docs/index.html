Space situational awareness from public data

Who is parked next to your satellite?

In geostationary orbit some satellites are built to inspect others. Russia's Luch/Olymp satellites move along the belt and stop a few tens of kilometres from communications satellites; China's SJ-21 towed a dead satellite away and later docked with SJ-25. From the public catalogue of orbits alone, I built a pipeline that detects their manoeuvres, finds where they park and measures how close they get. It finds 8 of the 8 satellites that public analyses name as Luch/Olymp-K targets, and its manoeuvre detector scores F1 0.90 against operator logs.

Four stories in the data

What the pipeline reconstructs for each inspector, told from the numbers. None of it needs inside information: it is all in the public catalogue, once the orbits are turned into manoeuvres, slots and distances.

Luch/Olymp-K · 2014 to 2025

Thirty-one stops along the belt

Luch/Olymp-K held station in 31 slots between 24°W and 96°E, almost always beside a communications satellite: 28 different ones within a median 100 km. Its typical stand-off is about 65 km, and almost entirely in-track: it shares the target's orbit, slightly ahead or behind, so radial and cross-track offsets stay below a kilometre on average.

Its last north-south manoeuvre was in November 2023; from then on its inclination grows freely, a common way to save fuel late in a mission. In October 2025 it was raised about 330 km above the belt, where it drifts west by 4.2° a day: consistent with retirement to a graveyard orbit.

Luch/Olymp 2 · since 2023

Parked beside Intelsat 39

Launched in March 2023, Luch/Olymp 2 picked up where its predecessor left off: Eutelsat KA-SAT and 9B, Eutelsat 3B and Konnect VHTS, Astra 4A, Thor 6 and 7, and Intelsat 10-02 together with the MEV-2 servicing vehicle docked to it.

Since March 2025 it has not moved from Intelsat 39 at 62°E: within 50 km for 459 of 582 days, a median 36 km away and slightly ahead of it.

SJ-21 · December 2021 to January 2022

A tow to the graveyard

Two months after its launch, SJ-21 reached the dead navigation satellite Beidou-2 G2 and from 30 December the two are tracked within a few kilometres of each other. On 25 January 2022 the pair appears about 1,700 km above the belt; two days later SJ-21 is back near GEO, while Beidou-2 G2 stays in an orbit between 300 and 3,100 km above it, where it still drifts west by 20° a day.

Between 11 and 21 January the two catalogue entries part by up to 2,000 km before coinciding again: from public data alone it is impossible to say whether the objects separated or one entry was mis-assigned.

SJ-21 and SJ-25 · 2025 to 2026

Two servicers, one object

SJ-25 was launched in January 2025. In June both satellites moved to about 127.5°E, and in mid-August the inclination of both dropped from 10.6° to 4.4° within a week: a 6.6° plane change, about 360 m/s, made while the two were reported to be docked.

In October and November 2025 they are a median 60 m apart, far below the TLE noise: the catalogue sees one object. By 7 Oct 2026 they are 497 km apart again.

Can the manoeuvre detector be trusted?

Nobody publishes an inspector's manoeuvres, so the detector was scored where the truth is known: five Fengyun weather satellites whose operator logged every burn between 2011 and 2022 (Shorten et al., 2023). Same code and same thresholds as for the inspectors.

A detection counts if it falls between one day before and four days after a logged burn: TLEs react late, because the catalogue needs several days of tracking to refit an orbit. Most detections that are "not in the log" are real: many are as large as the logged ones and clearly visible in the data, so the precision is a lower bound.

How it works

Five steps in Python, from the public catalogue to distances in kilometres. Every step is tested, and the code is on GitHub.

01

Download

The full TLE history of each object from Space-Track, cached and updated incrementally to stay inside the usage limits, plus the whole GEO belt around every parking date.

02

Mean elements

Each element set becomes a longitude, a drift rate and an inclination, propagating with SGP4 across one full orbit so that the daily oscillation cancels out.

03

Manoeuvres

A step in drift rate or inclination flags a burn, judged against the local noise with robust statistics; the size of the step gives the Δv.

04

Neighbours

Weeks at one longitude become a parking slot, and every catalogued object within half a degree of it becomes a candidate target.

05

Relative motion

Inspector and target are propagated together and the distance is resolved in the target's radial, in-track and cross-track frame, with the TLE error measured from the data.

What the public catalogue can't tell you

Public TLEs are coarse, late and occasionally wrong. Every number on this page comes with those limits, and where they matter the page says so.

  • Kilometres, not metres

    For the pairs on this page, public element sets place an object to within a few hundred metres to a few kilometres. A 40 km stand-off is solid; a median of 60 m only means "closer than the data can resolve", consistent with docking but not a measurement of it.

  • Most of a day late

    A burn shows up in the catalogue a median 0.7 days later, once enough tracking has been collected to refit the orbit.

  • Labels can slip

    When two objects are close, an element set can be attributed to the wrong one. The SJ-21 tow shows ten days of exactly that kind of ambiguity.

  • Positions, not intentions

    The data shows where a satellite went and how close it got. It does not show what it did there, and objects missing from the public catalogue are invisible.
